The paper proposes and evaluates a deterministic, sample-free method for estimating expected outputs of randomly initialized MLPs over Gaussian inputs, but does not describe, participate in, or report results for the ARC White-Box Estimation Challenge (WhestBench)[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. ## Estimation Method: Cumulant Propagation with Hermite Expansions

The core approach is a deterministic, sample-free cumulant propagation procedure[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Starting from the known Gaussian input distribution, the method advances low-order cumulant tensors through each layer of the network: linear (weight) layers contract cumulant tensors, and nonlinear activation layers update cumulants using truncated Hermite-based diagram formulas and power-cumulant computations[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The maximum tracked cumulant order \$K\$ is a user-chosen parameter that trades computation for accuracy; for odd \$K\$, the method additionally retains a scalar trace of the \$(K+1)\$-order cumulant tensor[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

Off-diagonal covariances are handled via a Hermite series in the cross-covariance of Gaussian-matched pre-activations, while diagonal variances are computed exactly through power cumulants[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. For piecewise-polynomial activations such as ReLU, Hermite coefficients reduce to Gaussian density and CDF evaluations, enabling semi-analytic computation[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

Four algorithmic variants are evaluated: basic, augmented, factorized, and factorized-augmented cumulant propagation[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Factorization applies for \$K \geq 3\$ and reduces the leading width-dependent term by a factor of \$n\$ while introducing quadratic depth dependence[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

The experiments span widths 4–256, depths 2–12, and cumulant orders \$K = 1\$–4[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. MSE is empirically proportional to \$1/n^K\$ at large width, but increasing \$K\$ can worsen results at small widths[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The paper conjectures an MSE bound of \$c_K(L/n)^K\$, with approximate \$L^K\$ growth at large \$L\$; Monte Carlo error does not increase with depth[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. A critical caveat is that despite the FLOP advantage, wall-clock times usually favor sampling due to inefficient Python operations in the proof-of-concept implementation[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

## Covariance-Response and Fourth-Cumulant Modeling

The research question specifically asks about covariance-response fourth-cumulant models. The paper does not describe a standalone fourth-cumulant covariance-response model as the primary source of gains[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Rather, cumulant propagation tracks cumulants through a user-specified maximum order \$K\$; fourth-order cumulants are included when \$K = 4\$ and are propagated layer by layer like the lower-order cumulants[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The covariance response at each activation layer is computed via a Hermite series in the cross-covariance of Gaussian-matched pre-activations, with leading-order terms used for off-diagonal entries and power cumulants providing exact diagonal variances[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The gains arise from the combined effect of tracking higher-order cumulants and using Hermite-based diagram summation at nonlinearities, rather than from a separable fourth-cumulant model[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

## Rare-Event Estimation

For rare-event probability estimation, the final ReLU is replaced by the threshold indicator \$\mathbb{1}\_{z\>3}\$, so the expected output equals a low-probability event probability[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. At FLOPs comparable to 5,695 Monte Carlo samples, the estimator achieved under 30% relative error for probabilities 100 times below \$1/N\$ (where \$N\$ is the MC sample count), and outperformed all baselines until probabilities were nearly 10,000 times below \$1/N\$[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

## Model Training via Mechanistic Distillation

The estimator was integrated into a training loop by providing differentiable, sample-free estimates of expected teacher–student squared-output error for gradient descent[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. In the reported experiment (width-16 teacher, width-8 student, four hidden layers, ReLU, basic \$K = 2\$ cumulant propagation), 256 gradient steps were compared with Monte Carlo SGD using batch size 36, chosen for comparable per-step FLOPs[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Mechanistic distillation successfully trained the student network but was slightly less efficient than SGD[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Mechanistic loss estimates are biased but variance-free, whereas MC estimates are unbiased but noisy[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The paper proposes catastrophic-event reduction as a potential safety benefit of mechanistic training but does not report a measured reduction in catastrophic events[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

## Synthesis

The single included source presents a coherent picture of the cumulant-propagation approach for wide random MLPs: at sufficient width and moderate depth, deterministic cumulant tracking outperforms Monte Carlo sampling in FLOP-normalized MSE by orders of magnitude[Wilson Wu et al., 2026], and the theoretical factor-\$n\$ speedup is supported by formal guarantees for polynomial activations at fixed depth[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The method degrades predictably when width is small relative to depth or when the tracked cumulant order \$K\$ exceeds what the width can support, consistent with the conjectured \$(L/n)^K\$ error scaling[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. These conditions delineate a clear applicability regime rather than an ambiguous boundary: for networks with width substantially exceeding depth (e.g., width 256, 2–4 hidden layers), the factorized estimator delivers large gains; at width 256 and 8 hidden layers with \$K = 4\$, sampling is preferable[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

The rare-event advantage is mechanistically coherent: Monte Carlo requires observing an event at least once to estimate its probability, which demands sample counts exceeding \$1/p\$, whereas cumulant propagation leverages distributional structure to extrapolate below the sampling resolution[Wilson Wu et al., 2026; Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. This advantage erodes at extremely low probabilities where accumulated approximation error dominates[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

Two important caveats temper the results. First, the rigorous theoretical guarantees cover polynomial activations only; extension to ReLU and other non-polynomial activations is empirically supported but analytically unproved[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. Second, the FLOP advantage does not currently translate to wall-clock speed in the authors' Python implementation[Wilson Wu et al., 2026], leaving open the question of whether optimized implementations would close this gap. The paper's training experiment demonstrates feasibility of mechanistic distillation but does not yet show it outperforming standard SGD or achieving measurable catastrophic-risk reduction[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].
