## Effects

The factorized cumulant propagation method achieves a factor-\$n\$ FLOP reduction relative to Monte Carlo at fixed depth by exploiting the factorization structure of cumulants at initialization[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The basic (unfactorized) method has time cost \$\Theta(n^{K+1})\$ per layer, where \$K\$ is the maximum cumulant order tracked, while factorization reduces this to \$O(n^K)\$[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. For ReLU networks specifically, Table 1 of the paper provides concrete FLOP polynomials for these implementations[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

The depth-dependent degradation is notable: at 8 hidden layers with cumulant order \$K = 4\$, the factorized method can underperform Monte Carlo[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. This degradation reflects a fundamental accuracy limitation tied to the accumulation of non-Gaussian structure with depth, which connects to the theoretical findings of several other sources.

### Rare-Event Estimation

Wilson Wu et al. (2026) additionally demonstrate that the cumulant propagation framework achieves under 30% relative error at probabilities 100 times below the reciprocal Monte Carlo sample count with a comparable FLOP budget[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. This rare-event capability represents a qualitative advantage over Monte Carlo, whose accuracy on tail probabilities requires sample counts inversely proportional to the target probability.

### Moment Matching Comparisons

Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin (2026) derive exact moment matching (mean and covariance propagation) for ReLU (as a limit of GeLU), GeLU, probit, Heaviside, and sine activations in feedforward and generalized residual layers[Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin, 2026]. On random networks, this second-order method achieves KL-divergence improvements of up to a millionfold over popular alternatives, and on a variational Bayes neural network, it attains hundredfold improvements over a state-of-the-art deterministic inference method relative to Monte Carlo ground truth[Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin, 2026]. Under regularity assumptions, moment matching removes leading low-variance errors and propagates higher-order local accuracy through layers[Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin, 2026]. However, this source is abstract-only, and the FLOP scaling is not explicitly reported in the available text[Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin, 2026].

### Distributional Approximation Quality

Lucia Celli (2026) establishes that multidimensional Edgeworth expansions of order \$4m - 1\$ approximate the law of finite-width network outputs (evaluated at a finite number of inputs) with total-variation error of order \$n^{-m}\$, and matching lower bounds confirm this rate is optimal under the stated assumptions (invertible Gaussian-limit covariance, polynomially bounded activation)[Lucia Celli, 2026]. When the infinite-width Gaussian prior is replaced by its Edgeworth expansion, the convergence rate and accuracy of the Bayesian posterior approximation in total variation improve[Lucia Celli, 2026]. This result provides the theoretical basis for understanding how many terms of an Edgeworth expansion are needed to achieve a given distributional accuracy for a network of width \$n\$.

### Finite-Width Correction Predictions

Gadi Naveh et al. (2020, 2020a) derive leading finite-width corrections scaling as \$1/N\$ for DNNs with arbitrary activation functions and depth under a noisy-gradient training protocol[Gadi Naveh et al., 2020; Gadi Naveh et al., 2020a]. These corrections predict empirical finite-network outputs with high accuracy, improving upon GP prediction accuracy by over an order of magnitude[Gadi Naveh et al., 2020a]. For CNNs, the leading correction captures weight-sharing effects and enables performance that can exceed the corresponding GP[Gadi Naveh et al., 2020]. These corrections are negligible for both very small and very large training sets[Gadi Naveh et al., 2020].

## Thematic Analysis

### Cumulant Hierarchies and Perturbative Structure

A central theme across the theoretical sources is that finite-width neural network statistics organize into perturbatively solvable hierarchies in powers of \$1/n\$. Boris Hanin (2022, 2022a) demonstrates that the \$k\$-th order cumulants at one layer depend at leading order only on cumulants of order \$j \leq k\$ at the previous layer[Boris Hanin, 2022; Boris Hanin, 2022a]. This hierarchical structure is what makes layer-by-layer cumulant propagation tractable: one can truncate at a chosen order \$K\$ and propagate recursively without needing to solve for all orders simultaneously[Boris Hanin, 2022].

Sho Yaida (2019) develops a complementary approach, tracking preactivation distributions by progressively integrating out random variables from lower to higher layers, yielding non-Gaussian process priors for finite-width networks[Sho Yaida, 2019]. This layer-by-layer integration is described as reminiscent of renormalization-group flow[Sho Yaida, 2019], a connection made more explicit in the field-theoretic treatments.

### The Role of Depth-to-Width Ratio

Multiple sources identify the depth-to-width ratio \$L/n\$ as the effective parameter controlling finite-width deviations. Hanin (2022, 2022a) shows that while cumulant recursions form a hierarchy in powers of \$1/n\$, contributions of order \$1/n^k\$ often grow like \$L^k\$, making them non-negligible at positive \$L/n\$[Boris Hanin, 2022; Boris Hanin, 2022a]. The depth-to-width ratio controls both single-neuron fluctuations and inter-neuron correlations[Boris Hanin, 2022a], and a simplified variant of the exploding/vanishing gradient problem occurs if and only if \$L/n\$ is large[Boris Hanin, 2022a].

This finding directly explains the depth-dependent degradation observed in Wilson Wu et al. (2026): at 8 hidden layers with moderate width, the accumulated cumulant contributions become large enough to overwhelm the truncation at \$K = 4\$[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. The theoretical prediction that \$1/n^k\$ terms grow as \$L^k\$[Boris Hanin, 2022] means that for a fixed truncation order, the error grows polynomially (or worse) with depth, eventually negating the FLOP advantage of the deterministic method.

Grosvenor & Jefferson (2021) arrive at analogous conclusions from a field-theoretic perspective, computing \$O(1)\$ ensemble-fluctuation and \$O(T/N)\$ finite-width corrections to two-point correlations[Kevin T. Grosvenor & R. Jefferson, 2021]. Non-Gaussian effects accumulate with depth and are suppressed by width[Kevin T. Grosvenor & R. Jefferson, 2021], and these corrections modify the effective correlation length that sets the depth scale for trainability[Kevin T. Grosvenor & R. Jefferson, 2021]. However, their perturbative results show no shift in the critical point and are valid only in a weak-coupling region that breaks down at the edge of chaos[Kevin T. Grosvenor & R. Jefferson, 2021].

### Exact Distributional Methods and Their Limitations

Burkholz & Dubatovka (2018) derive the output distribution exactly (without mean-field assumptions) for fully connected networks with Gaussian weights and biases, characterizing layer-wise signal-distribution transitions via a linear integral operator with a closed-form kernel for ReLU[R. Burkholz & Alina Dubatovka, 2018]. This exact approach avoids the truncation errors inherent in cumulant or moment methods, but the computational cost of working with the full integral operator may limit practical scalability, and the source is abstract-only so detailed cost analysis is unavailable.

### Field-Theoretic and Renormalization Group Perspectives

The NN–QFT correspondence provides an alternative lens on finite-width corrections. Erbin et al. (2021) develop a non-perturbative RG formalism in which the weight-distribution standard deviation acts as a running cutoff governing coupling flow[Harold Erbin et al., 2021]. This interpretation—that changing the initialization scale amounts to a renormalization flow in network space—offers a principled way to organize corrections beyond the leading \$1/n\$ order. However, this analysis focuses on translation-invariant Gauss-nets rather than the ReLU MLP cumulant methods central to the research question[Harold Erbin et al., 2021].

### Infinite-Width Baseline and Its Insufficiency

Lee et al. (2017) establish the exact GP equivalence for infinitely wide deep networks and show that GP predictions typically outperform finite-width networks[Jaehoon Lee et al., 2017]. This finding sets the baseline: the infinite-width GP provides an efficient deterministic prediction, but it misses all finite-width structure. As width increases, trained-network accuracy approaches GP accuracy[Jaehoon Lee et al., 2017], but for practical finite widths, the corrections described by the other sources are essential.

## Synthesis

The central question—whether deterministic methods can estimate expected ReLU MLP outputs more cheaply than Monte Carlo—receives a conditionally affirmative answer that depends critically on the depth-to-width ratio. The apparent tension between the large speedups reported at shallow depths[Wilson Wu et al., 2026] and the degradation or failure at greater depths[Wilson Wu et al., 2026] is explained by a consistent mechanism identified across multiple theoretical sources.

At shallow depths (2–4 hidden layers) and moderate-to-large widths (e.g., \$n = 256\$), the factorized cumulant propagation method achieves 10- to 1,000-fold FLOP savings over Monte Carlo[Wilson Wu et al., 2026], because the non-Gaussian corrections remain small and well-captured by low-order (\$K \leq 4\$) cumulant truncation. The factorization itself provides the dominant cost reduction, lowering per-layer cost from \$\Theta(n^{K+1})\$ to \$O(n^K)\$[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].

At greater depths, the \$1/n^k\$ cumulant contributions grow as \$L^k\$[Boris Hanin, 2022; Boris Hanin, 2022a], which means the effective expansion parameter is \$L/n\$ rather than \$1/n\$ alone. When \$L/n\$ is non-negligible, higher-order cumulants that are neglected by truncation at order \$K\$ become significant, and the deterministic estimator's error exceeds Monte Carlo's statistical error for a comparable FLOP budget[Wilson Wu et al., 2026]. This is not a deficiency of the specific implementation but a fundamental property of the cumulant hierarchy itself: the non-Gaussianity generated at ReLU kinks accumulates layer by layer, and each additional layer injects new non-Gaussian structure that propagates forward.

The Edgeworth expansion framework of Celli (2026) provides a complementary perspective: to achieve total-variation error \$O(n^{-m})\$, one needs an expansion of order \$4m - 1\$[Lucia Celli, 2026], and this rate is tight (matching lower bounds exist)[Lucia Celli, 2026]. For networks of moderate width, achieving high distributional accuracy thus requires high-order expansions, whose computational cost grows with the expansion order. The moment-matching approach of Kuang & Lin (2026) takes a different route by propagating only mean and covariance exactly, achieving dramatic KL-divergence improvements (up to millionfold) over alternatives that approximate these moments[Simon Kuang & Xinfan Lin, 2026], but this second-order method does not capture the higher cumulants that become important at depth.

For specific contexts, the following conclusions can be drawn:

- For shallow networks (depth \$\ll\$ width), factorized cumulant propagation at \$K = 2\$–\$4\$ offers substantial and reliable FLOP savings over Monte Carlo, with the factorized method scaling as \$O(n^K)\$ per layer versus \$O(n^2/\varepsilon^2)\$ total for sampling[Wilson Wu et al., 2026; Wilson Wu et al., 2026].
- For networks where \$L/n\$ is appreciable, the cumulant truncation error becomes the bottleneck. Increasing \$K\$ raises the per-layer FLOP cost as \$O(n^K)\$[Wilson Wu et al., 2026] while providing diminishing accuracy returns because contributions of order \$1/n^k\$ grow as \$L^k\$[Boris Hanin, 2022].
- For rare-event estimation, the cumulant propagation approach retains a qualitative advantage regardless of depth concerns, since Monte Carlo requires sample counts that scale inversely with the target probability[Wilson Wu et al., 2026].
- The \$1/N\$ finite-width corrections derived by Naveh et al. (2020, 2020a) provide an alternative deterministic correction that is particularly effective for trained networks, improving prediction accuracy by over an order of magnitude relative to GP baselines[Gadi Naveh et al., 2020a], though these address a different computational setting (post-training prediction rather than initialization-time expected output estimation).
