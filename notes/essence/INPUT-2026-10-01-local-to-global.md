## Message 2 (the other chat's take, pasted by the user: "also this chats take may contain some useful insights or spark ones to persue")

> The second paper is Jeronimo–Mittal–Roy, "Pseudorandomness of expander walks via Fourier analysis on groups" (July 2025). Your instinct about it is right in a literal sense. Its key proof technique, a generalized "ignore first step" trick, deletes the first vertex of a walk and reduces a length-k problem to a length-(k−1) problem on the squared graph. That is the same peel-one-off move as the JSV permanent chain's "remove an edge, slide the hole, re-add an edge."
> Below I state the essence as one piece of mathematics and show how far it reaches. I then say where noncommutative geometry (NCG) genuinely enters, including a classical result I think is the cleanest bridge you're after, and separate where noncommutativity pays off computationally from where it backfires.
>
> **1. The essence, stated exactly**
> Every instance has the same skeleton:
> * a down step that forgets one piece (a conditional expectation onto everything except that piece);
> * an up step that re-randomizes or reconstructs it;
> * a global behaviour (mixing, recovery, approximation) controlled by how far the "forget this" and "forget that" subspaces are from being independent.
>
> That distance is measured by an angle. Take two subspaces with orthogonal projections P and Q. Halmos's two-subspace theorem splits everything into 2×2 blocks indexed by angles θ. In each block:
> * Alternating projections, (PQ)ⁿ, contract by cos²θ per round. This is mixing (von Neumann's alternating projections), and cos θ is the maximal correlation between the two pieces of information.
> * Alternating reflections 2P−1 and 2Q−1 rotate by 2θ. This is periodicity when θ/π is rational and quasi-periodicity when it isn't.
>
> The universal C*-algebra generated by two projections is the group algebra of the infinite dihedral group, and its irreducible representations are indexed by exactly these angles. So the noncommutative space behind every "forget one, put it back" process is an interval of angles, and each process is a measure on that interval.
>
> **2. Where it lives**
> Each item below is the same down–up skeleton:
> * JSV permanent chain: delete an edge from a perfect matching (leaving two holes), slide a hole, re-add an edge. Canonical paths bound the angle (the conductance) inverse-polynomially.
> * Jeronimo–Mittal–Roy: drop the first step of the walk. Because irreducible representations are unitary, the dropped vertex contributes only a norm-one factor, and the expander mixing lemma (an angle bound) closes the recursion. The noncommutative part is Fourier analysis over nonabelian groups: matrix-valued irreps and Clebsch–Gordan fusion.
> * Glauber dynamics: forget one spin and resample it from its conditional law. Spectral independence bounds the local angles, and the Alev–Lau and Oppenheim local-to-global theorems turn them into a global gap.
> * Matroid bases: drop one element, add one (the basis-exchange walk of Mihail–Vazirani's conjecture). Here "drop element i" is literally the partial derivative ∂ᵢ of the basis polynomial, and the local check is the signature of a quadratic form (Anari–Liu–Oveis Gharan–Vinzant; Brändén–Huh's Lorentzian polynomials).
> * Localization schemes (Chen–Eldan): pin one coordinate at a time, or tilt continuously (stochastic localization). This unifies spectral independence with the technique behind KLS bounds and diffusion-model sampling.
> * Gaussian elimination: eliminating one vertex is a Schur complement, i.e. marginalizing a Gaussian, and it creates a clique. Kyng–Sachdeva replace that clique with a random sparse sample, which yields near-linear-time Laplacian solvers. The same sparsify-and-recurse philosophy, with expander decompositions, underlies almost-linear-time max-flow.
> * Chen–Rouzé: erase region A, run a detailed-balanced Lindbladian on A, then time-average.
> * Sinkhorn and operator scaling: alternately normalize rows and columns (Kraus operators in the quantum case). These are two alternating conditional expectations.
> * Cluster mutation: remove one cluster variable and the exchange relation supplies the new one. The bipartite belt alternates white and black vertices — two reflections.
> * Tilings: flip chains remove a hexagon of three rhombi and re-insert it rotated. Coupling from the past gives exact samples via the height-function lattice.
> * Jones' tower: add one projection at a time. The Markov trace satisfies tr(x eₙ) = τ tr(x) for x in the earlier algebra, so the new projection behaves as if independent of the past.
>
> **3. The noncommutative core, and a bridge that already exists**
> Three facts make the NCG version precise.
> Commuting squares are conditional independence. For von Neumann subalgebras N and Q with conditional expectations, the Popa commuting-square condition `E_N E_Q = E_{N∩Q}` says N and Q are independent given N∩Q: the angle is 90°. Approximate commuting squares give quasi-factorization of relative entropy (Gao–Junge–LaRacuente; Bardet–Capel–Rouzé), which is how quantum Gibbs samplers are shown to mix. This is the noncommutative form of "local angles ⇒ global mixing."
> Coxeter functors govern systems of projections. This is the bridge I'd point to first.
> * Kruglyak and Roiter proved a Gabriel-type theorem for representations of graphs in Hilbert spaces: a graph has finitely many indecomposable representations of this kind if and only if it is Dynkin.
> * Kruglyak–Rabanovich–Samoilenko determined exactly when n projections can sum to αI. They used a linear reflection and a hyperbolic reflection, whose composites they call Coxeter functors. The admissible α come from continued fractions. For n ≥ 5 there is a continuous band `[(n−√(n²−4n))/2, (n+√(n²−4n))/2]`; for five projections its edges are 1+φ⁻² and 1+φ².
>
> The same reflection-functor machinery that classifies systems of projections — the noncommutative skeleton of down–up processes — also drives Chin's bipartite belt. For a Dynkin quiver the belt is exactly the Coxeter functor (the inverse Auslander–Reiten translation).
> The bipartite Coxeter element is literally a product of two reflections, s◦s•. By A'Campo, its eigenvalues λ satisfy λ + λ⁻¹ + 2 = μ², where μ runs over adjacency eigenvalues. Writing μ = 2cos θ turns this into rotation by 2θ:
> * μ < 2 (Dynkin): rational angles πm/h give periodicity (Zamolodchikov).
> * μ = 2 (affine): unipotent behaviour, linear growth, integrable.
> * μ > 2 (wild): imaginary angles and exponential growth. This is where expanders live, and where probabilistic local-to-global arguments (mixing, pseudo-randomness) take over from exact algebra.
>
> The same wall at 2 shows up repeatedly:
> * Jones index 4: subfactor indices below 4 are exactly 4cos²(π/n), with Dynkin principal graphs.
> * Smith's theorem: a connected graph has spectral radius below 2 exactly when it is a simply-laced Dynkin diagram.
> * Kruglyak–Roiter: Hilbert-space representations of a graph are finite in number exactly in the Dynkin case.
>
> Connes' Penrose subfactor sits at index φ² = 4cos²(π/5), just below the wall. My reading — a synthesis, not a theorem — is that spectral radius 2 (Jones index 4) marks where local-to-global stops being exact algebra (periodicity, Laurent phenomena, Kasteleyn) and has to become probabilistic (expansion, mixing).
> Quasirandomness: noncommutativity can manufacture pseudo-randomness. In Gowers' quasirandom groups — groups with no small nontrivial irreps — products of large sets are already close to uniform. The same multiplicity effect produces expanders (Sarnak–Xue, Bourgain–Gamburd). Jeronimo–Mittal–Roy's improved bounds for D-quasirandom groups are this effect applied to expander walks.
>
> **4. Where noncommutativity pays off computationally, and where it backfires**
> Where it pays:
> * Operator scaling. The noncommutative version of Edmonds' problem (the rank of a symbolic matrix over the free skew field) is in deterministic polynomial time via alternating normalization (Garg–Gurvits–Oliveira–Wigderson). Derandomizing its commutative cousin is a major open problem tied to circuit lower bounds. A spectral gap makes the alternation converge fast (Kwok–Lau–Ramachandran). What noncommutativity buys here is symmetry and geodesic convexity.
> * Noncommutative randomization of the permanent. Replace the random ±1 signs of the Godsil–Gutman estimator with random Clifford-algebra elements. Chien–Rasmussen–Sinclair show the variance ratio becomes bounded by a constant once the algebra has polynomial dimension. The quaternion case is computable in polynomial time with ratio (3/2)^{n/2}, exponentially better than the complex-number version.
> * Anyons at the Penrose index. Index φ² is the Fibonacci-anyon index. Fibonacci braid representations are dense in the unitary group of each finite stage of Connes' Penrose algebra (Freedman–Larsen–Wang). Additively approximating the Jones polynomial at e^{2πi/5} is BQP-complete, while computing it exactly is #P-hard. So the algebra behind the turn-two NCG example carries universal quantum computation at its natural index.
>
> Where it backfires:
> * The sign problem migrates. The Clifford estimators need the noncommutative determinant. Chien–Harsha–Sinclair–Srinivasan proved a dichotomy for a fixed finite-dimensional algebra A over a finite field: the determinant is easy if A modulo its radical is commutative, and as hard as the permanent otherwise. Over any field, 2×2 matrix entries already make it as hard as the permanent. Moore–Russell show the variance stays exponential for constant-dimensional algebras.
> * No quantum local-to-global theorem yet. A 2025 paper on quantum Gibbs sampling via noncommutative transport states outright that, unlike classically, no general iterable approximate tensorization is known quantumly. Existing results cover product fixed points, 1D, nearest-neighbour interactions, high temperature, or weak interactions.
> * Local rules can encode computation. Robinson's hierarchical tiling is how Cubitt–Pérez-García–Wolf made the spectral gap undecidable.
> * Finite-matrix approximation fails. Connes' embedding problem was refuted via MIP* = RE, whose engine is local testing: local consistency forcing global structure.
>
> The lesson: noncommutativity pays when it supplies symmetry or convexity (scaling, quasirandomness, braiding). It backfires when you need it to cancel signs.
>
> **5. Where I'd look for the large payoff**
> 1. A noncommutative trickle-down theorem. State the Garland / Oppenheim / Alev–Lau theorems for lattices of generalized (Petz-type) conditional expectations, with local angles measured by approximate commuting squares, so that they yield iterable approximate tensorization for quantum Gibbs states. This is exactly the gap named above. The payoff would be provable rapid mixing of quantum Gibbs samplers beyond perturbative regimes — certified quantum advantage for thermal-state preparation. Yang's CMI bounds are a natural input, since (as in last turn's Gaussian identity) CMI is the entropic form of the angle.
> 2. Noncommutative localization schemes. Continuous weak measurement produces a martingale of density matrices that localizes (quantum filtering), a natural analogue of stochastic localization. The question is whether approximate conservation of quantum entropy along such a process implies a modified log-Sobolev inequality for an associated quantum down–up channel. That would import Chen–Eldan's toolkit, including diffusion-style samplers, into quantum Gibbs sampling. I haven't found this in the literature.
> 3. Pseudo-randomness beyond groups. Extend Jeronimo–Mittal–Roy from group labels to fusion-category labels, with Fibonacci as the test case: fusion coefficients replace Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, and global dimension replaces |G|. The question is whether expander walks fool fusion-symmetric functions with error governed by quantum dimensions. This would tie expander pseudo-randomness directly to the Penrose/Jones/anyon structure.
> 4. Beating sign migration on structured inputs. The dichotomy rules out general noncommutative determinants. Planar, bounded-genus or expander matrices might still admit algebras where variance reduction and efficient determinants coexist. Mapping that frontier is a concrete question with the permanent and boson sampling at stake.
